Domain 1
Impaired control
Criteria 1 – 4
C1
Uses more, or for longer, than intended
Exam hook: The intention–reality gap. Plans one drink, has six. Common first sign. Patient often least wants to admit this criterion because it implies loss of control.
C2
Persistent desire or repeated failed attempts to cut down or stop
Exam hook: The desire exists — this is not denial. The behaviour continues despite repeated attempts to stop. Multiple failed quit attempts distinguish it from simple ambivalence.
C3
Great deal of time spent obtaining, using, or recovering from the substance
Exam hook: Substance-related activity consumes hours or days. Time previously spent on work, family, or leisure is now lost to the use cycle. Key functional impairment indicator.
C4
Craving or strong urge to use
Exam hook: New in DSM-5 — not present in DSM-IV. Neurobiological basis: conditioned dopamine anticipation. Also the theoretical target of cue-exposure therapy.
Domain 2
Social impairment
Criteria 5 – 7
C5
Failure to fulfil major role obligations (work, school, home)
Exam hook: Missing work, failing exams, neglecting children. Substance now competes with role responsibilities — and wins. Functional impairment, not just use frequency.
C6
Continued use despite persistent social or interpersonal problems caused by the substance
Exam hook: Knows the relationship is damaged by substance use — continues anyway. The awareness of harm + continuation is what makes this clinically significant, not just the harm alone.
C7
Important social, occupational, or recreational activities given up or reduced because of use
Exam hook: Hobbies, sports, friendships, professional activities abandoned. Substance monopolises the reinforcement landscape. Reflects both positive and negative reinforcement narrowing life to the drug.
Domain 3
Risky use
Criteria 8 – 9
C8
Recurrent use in physically hazardous situations
Exam hook: Driving under the influence, operating machinery. Key word: recurrent. A single lapse is not sufficient — a pattern of hazardous use is required for this criterion.
C9
Continued use despite knowing it causes or worsens a physical or psychological problem
Exam hook: Drinks knowing it exacerbates liver disease. Uses opioids knowing it triggers depression. The knowledge + continuation is the criterion — not ignorance, not denial.
Domain 4
Pharmacological
Criteria 10 – 11
C10
Tolerance — markedly increased amounts needed for same effect, or markedly diminished effect with same amount
Exam trap: Tolerance occurring solely within prescribed therapeutic use (e.g. opioids for pain, benzos for anxiety) does NOT count unless compulsive use is also present.
C11
Withdrawal — characteristic syndrome on cessation, or use to relieve or avoid withdrawal symptoms
Exam traps: Same medical-context exemption as C10. Hallucinogens: no withdrawal criterion. Caffeine: withdrawal diagnosable but no SUD diagnosis. Tobacco: withdrawal ✓ but no intoxication criterion.
2 – 3
Mild
Minimum threshold. Often first clinical presentation. Many patients do not recognise this as a disorder.
4 – 5
Moderate
Significant impairment across multiple domains. Usually affecting relationships and work.
6 +
Severe
Most criteria met. High medical and social burden. Often associated with polysubstance use and comorbidities.
DSM-IV → DSM-5
DSM-IV had separate Abuse (1+ criteria) and Dependence (3+ criteria) diagnoses. DSM-5 unified them into a single SUD spectrum and added craving (C4). The term addiction was removed due to stigma and uncertain definition.
Caffeine — the only exclusion
Caffeine is the only substance class with no SUD diagnosis in DSM-5. Caffeine intoxication (≥5 symptoms after 250 mg+) and caffeine withdrawal (headache, fatigue, irritability on cessation) are both diagnosable — but there is no caffeine use disorder.
Medical-context rule (C10 + C11)
Tolerance and withdrawal occurring solely within appropriate medical treatment do not count toward SUD. The clinician must distinguish therapeutic dependence (expected pharmacological adaptation) from compulsive, problematic use. The presence of compulsive use tips both C10 and C11 back into play.
Substance classes covered (9 of 10)
SUD applies to: alcohol, cannabis, hallucinogens, inhalants, opioids, sedatives/hypnotics/anxiolytics, stimulants, tobacco, other/unknown. Caffeine excluded. Note: hallucinogens have no withdrawal criterion; tobacco has no intoxication criterion.
Self-medication hypothesis (comorbidity link)
SUD often develops secondary to another disorder — the individual uses the substance to cope with an underlying condition (anxiety, depression, PTSD, chronic pain). AUD and drug dependence are 2× more likely in individuals with anxiety, affective, or psychotic disorders.
